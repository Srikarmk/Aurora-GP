Allocating approximation budget unevenly across the input space, routing "important"
regions to high-fidelity approximations and the rest to cheap ones, is an appealing way
to scale Gaussian processes. We give a quantitative criterion for when it can work, and
show the criterion is rarely satisfied.

The criterion follows from the predictive variance decomposition: sparse approximations
alter only the latent term, leaving the shared noise term untouched. The gain available to
any region-aware router is therefore bounded by the spread in predictive standard deviation
across its candidate models, which is measurable before a router is built. On data whose
regional structure is true by construction, that spread is at most 0.0017 against a
calibration error of order 0.5. The criterion predicts both outcomes: where the spread is
negligible, an oracle router given the true partition gains nothing (+0.0003 ECE); where it
is larger (0.064), an oracle does gain, by the margin the spread permits. This holds from
n=600 to n=4800.

We contribute three further results. (i) A measurement hazard: an opt-in fast
predictive-variance setting in a widely used GP library is inert below a training-set size
threshold that never appears in user code, and above it perturbs predictive standard
deviations by a median of about 100%, inflating measured ECE by a factor of three to nine.
(ii) Region identification by local residual variance, recovering a known partition 96.9%
of the time where uncertainty-and-sparsity scoring manages 41% against 33% chance. (iii)
Shrinkage-regularised regional noise, whose empirical-Bayes form requires no tuning and is
the only arm to improve CRPS at every sample size tested.

We also report the research process: four of five explanatory mechanisms we proposed were
falsified by their own predictions, while claims resting on direct measurement survived
adversarial audit.
