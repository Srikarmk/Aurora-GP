Region-aware Gaussian processes adapt the model to different parts of the input space. One appealing form allocates approximation budget unevenly, routing "important" regions to high-fidelity sparse approximations and the rest to cheap ones. We show this form cannot change the uncertainty a region receives, and that a second form, adapting the noise model by region, can.

The reason is the predictive variance decomposition: sparse approximations alter only the latent term, while the noise term is shared. On four constructed datasets with known regional structure, models spanning a wide range of fidelity emit mean predictive standard deviations within 2% of one another in every region, while the true noise varies 50x across regions. An oracle router given the true generative partition gains nothing (+0.0003 ECE), and on five standard benchmarks importance routing is significantly worse than a random-routing control on two. Making the noise term input-dependent instead, with an unbiased out-of-fold noise estimate, pays: regional noise improves calibration significantly on five of eleven datasets and worsens it on none, and a heteroscedastic GP improves NLL on eight. The gains appear where measured residual variance is input-dependent and the training set is not tiny; the result replicates on disjoint seeds and survives five adversarial checks.

We also report a measurement hazard: a fast predictive-variance setting in a widely used GP library is inert below a training-set size threshold that never appears in user code, and above it perturbs predictive standard deviations by a median of about 100%, inflating measured ECE by a factor of three to ten at n=4000. Finally we report the research process: five of six mechanisms we proposed did not survive, and five headline numbers, including an earlier, opposite conclusion of this paper, turned on how a baseline or estimator was configured.
