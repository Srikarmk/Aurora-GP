One appealing form of region-aware Gaussian process routes "important" regions to high-fidelity sparse approximations and the rest to cheap ones. We find that, for the approximations we test (random Fourier features and Nystrom, each with its own homoscedastic noise), this form barely changes the uncertainty a region receives, and that adapting the noise model by region does change it.

The reason is the predictive variance decomposition: these approximations alter only the latent term, while the noise term, fitted separately by each model, comes out nearly the same. Varying Nystrom rank on five benchmarks changes test RMSE by up to 155% but mean predictive standard deviation by at most 12%; on four constructed datasets, four models' per-region predictive standard deviations agree within 3% while the true noise varies 50x. An oracle router given the true partition gains nothing on average (+0.0003 ECE). Making the noise term input-dependent instead, with a latent-variance-corrected out-of-fold noise estimate, pays on the larger datasets with clearly input-dependent residual variance: after Holm correction regional noise improves ECE on the same three datasets in both of two disjoint sets of ten splits and worsens it on none, though on the smallest dataset it worsens NLL and CRPS. The result survives six adversarial checks.

We also report a measurement hazard: an opt-in fast predictive-variance setting in a widely used GP library is inert below a training-set size threshold that never appears in user code, and above it perturbs predictive standard deviations by a median of about 100%, inflating ECE three- to tenfold at n=4000. Finally we report the research process: five of six mechanisms we proposed did not survive, and five headline numbers, including an earlier, opposite conclusion of this paper, turned on how a baseline or estimator was configured.
