\begin{abstract}
Region-aware Gaussian processes adapt the model to different parts of the input
space. One appealing form allocates approximation budget unevenly, routing
``important'' regions to high-fidelity sparse approximations and the rest to cheap
ones. We find that, for the approximations we test (random Fourier features and
Nystr\"om, each with its own homoscedastic noise), this form barely changes the
uncertainty a region receives, and that a second form, adapting the noise model by
region, does change it.

The reason is the predictive variance decomposition: these approximations alter only
the latent term, while the noise term, fitted separately by each model, comes out
nearly the same. Varying Nystr\"om rank on five benchmarks changes test RMSE by up to
$155\%$ but mean predictive standard deviation by at most $12\%$; on four constructed
datasets, four models' per-region predictive standard deviations agree within $3\%$ in
every region and seed while the true noise varies $50\times$. An oracle router given
the true generative partition gains nothing on average ($+0.0003$ ECE). Making the noise
term input-dependent instead, with a latent-variance-corrected out-of-fold noise
estimate, pays on the larger datasets with clearly input-dependent residual variance:
after Holm correction regional noise improves ECE on the same three datasets in both
of two disjoint sets of ten splits (four in the second set, five when pooled) and
worsens it on none, though on the
smallest dataset it worsens NLL and CRPS; a heteroscedastic GP improves NLL on seven
to nine. The result survives six adversarial checks, including recalibrating the
global baseline and a sweep over training-set size.

We also report a measurement hazard: an opt-in fast predictive-variance setting in a
widely used GP library is inert below a training-set size threshold that never
appears in user code, and above it perturbs predictive standard deviations by a
median of ${\sim}100\%$, inflating measured ECE by a factor of three to ten at
$n=4000$. Finally we report the research process (\cref{sec:process}): five of six
mechanisms we proposed did not survive, and five headline numbers, including an
earlier, opposite conclusion of this paper, turned on how a \emph{baseline} or
estimator was configured. We distil these into a checklist for GP calibration studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Exact Gaussian process regression costs $O(n^3)$, and a large literature reduces this
with sparse approximations~\citep{rasmussen2006, quinonero2005, titsias2009}. Most
such methods apply a single approximation uniformly across the input space. It is
natural to ask whether the budget should instead be allocated unevenly: regions that
are sparsely sampled, or where the model is uncertain, might warrant a more faithful
approximation than regions that are dense and easy.

We call this \emph{region-aware approximation}. The premise is appealing and the
mechanism is intuitive. This paper shows the mechanism does not deliver on the
premise, for a reason that does not depend on the partitioning criterion and holds for
every homoscedastic approximation we tested: approximation fidelity and predictive
uncertainty are nearly orthogonal. The same reason points to what does work. The term
of the predictive variance that can differ by region is the noise, and adapting it by
region improves calibration where the data warrant it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fidelity and uncertainty are nearly orthogonal}
(\cref{sec:orth,sec:routing}). Changing Nystr\"om rank moves accuracy far more than
predictive uncertainty, and models of nominally different fidelity emit per-region
predictive standard deviations within $3\%$ of one another on our constructed
datasets, so routing among them barely changes a region's uncertainty; oracle routing
gains nothing on average, and the importance criterion of the method we audited is
significantly worse than random routing on two of five benchmarks.
\item \textbf{Input-dependent noise pays on the larger datasets with clearly
input-dependent residual variance} (\cref{sec:noise}). With a latent-variance-corrected
out-of-fold noise estimate, regional noise beats a single global noise parameter on
$\ece$ on airfoil, sarcos and calhousing in both seed sets after Holm correction and
under a resampling-corrected test, and survives global recalibration (uncorrected) and,
where tested, a sweep over training-set size; a two-stage heteroscedastic GP improves
NLL on most datasets.
\item \textbf{A measurement hazard} (\cref{sec:hazard}) that silently corrupts
calibration metrics for ``exact'' GPs above a library default, by up to a factor of
ten, and is controlled by a setting other than the one that names it.
\item \textbf{A record of what failed and what caught it} (\cref{sec:process}),
including a reversal of this paper's own earlier conclusion, distilled into a
checklist for GP calibration studies.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Sparse GP approximations.} Given data $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with
$y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_n^2)$, a GP prior
$f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0,k)$ yields a predictive distribution at $x_*$ with variance
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:var}
\mathbb{V}[y_*] \;=\; \underbrace{k(x_*,x_*) - k_*^\top (K+\sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} k_*}_{\text{latent, approximation-dependent}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sigma_n^2}_{\text{input-independent}}.
\end{equation}
Random Fourier Features~\citep{rahimi2007} and Nystr\"om / subset-of-regressors
\citep{williams2001, smola2000} replace the first term with a rank-$m$ surrogate and
keep the second a single constant. (FITC~\citep{snelson2006} and
PITC~\citep{quinonero2005} are an exception: their (block-)diagonal correction acts as
input-dependent noise.) \Cref{sec:orth} shows this is what
dooms region-aware approximation.

\paragraph{Calibration metrics.} For regression we use the expected calibration error
over central predictive intervals,
\begin{equation}
\ece = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \Big| \tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_i \mathbf{1}\big[|y_i-\mu_i| \le z_{(1+c_b)/2}\,\sigma_i\big] - c_b \Big|,
\end{equation}
with $c_b$ spanning $[0.1,0.9]$, alongside Gaussian negative log-likelihood. Both
depend on $\sigma_i$, which is what makes~\cref{sec:hazard} consequential.

\section{A measurement hazard in approximate predictive variance}
\label{sec:hazard}

Before reporting calibration comparisons we must establish that they measure the
model rather than the inference path.

GPyTorch~\citep{gardner2018} provides \texttt{fast\_pred\_var()}, an implementation of
LOVE~\citep{pleiss2018} that approximates the predictive covariance, and separately
falls back from Cholesky to iterative solves when the training set exceeds
\texttt{max\_cholesky\_size} (default $800$). We fit one model per cell and vary
\emph{only} the inference path, clearing the model's prediction cache before each path:
GPyTorch caches the predictive covariance on first use, and a later call with
different settings silently reuses it. All measurements use GPyTorch 1.15.2 with
linear\_operator 0.6.1.

\caption{Median relative error in the predictive standard deviation against exact

A four-way decomposition isolates the cause. With the default settings the median
error in $\sigma$ across the $16$ cells with $n>800$ is $101.92\%$. Raising
\texttt{max\_cholesky\_size} above $n$ alone removes it ($0.00\%$; at most $0.01\%$ in
any cell), because LOVE then builds its low-rank factorisation from a Cholesky
decomposition rather than Lanczos iterations. Disabling \texttt{fast\_pred\_var()}
alone reduces the median to $5.62\%$ but leaves the error of the iterative solves, up
to $38\%$ (sarcos, $n=1000$). The approximation is therefore silently inert on small
data and silently severe on large data, with no change in user code, and the setting
that controls it is the Cholesky threshold rather than the flag that names it.
GPyTorch's fits are not bit-reproducible across runs: in two further independent runs
of this experiment the median error above the threshold was $100.3\%$ and $102.0\%$, the
maximum at or below it $0.0018\%$, and the $\ece$ ratio at $n=4000$ ranged from $2.9$ to
$10.2$; individual cells vary more (\texttt{FINDINGS.md}).

\paragraph{Implication.} Reported $\ece$ or NLL for ``exact'' GPs trained above
$n=800$ in this library may describe an approximation. Raising the Cholesky threshold
above $n$ suffices for the predictive variance; we recommend that calibration studies
also disable fast predictive variance, and report both settings.

\section{Fidelity and uncertainty are nearly orthogonal}
\label{sec:orth}

\Cref{eq:var} suggests that if $\sigma_n^2$ dominates, then changing the rank of the
approximation, which affects only the first term, barely moves the predictive
standard deviation. We test this in two ways: by varying fidelity over a wide range on
real data with everything else fixed, and by measuring, region by region, how much the
predictive uncertainty of differently approximated models differs on data with known
regional structure.

\paragraph{Accuracy moves; uncertainty does not.} \Cref{tab:ranksweep} varies only the
Nystr\"om rank, from $25$ to $1600$ ($400$ on concrete), with kernel and noise
hyperparameters fitted once by exact marginal likelihood and shared by every rank
(\texttt{src/mechanism.py}). Over this range the lowest-rank test RMSE is
$21$--$155\%$ above the highest-rank one, while mean predictive standard deviation
differs by $0$--$12\%$. Low-rank models are less accurate but not correspondingly less
certain; their $z$-scores are over-dispersed (\cref{tab:mechanisms}, first row).

\paragraph{Region by region.} We fit four models, RFF with $1000$ features, Nystr\"om

deviation varies $50\times$ across three regions by construction. Each model fits its

In every region of all four datasets and on every seed, the four models' mean
predictive standard deviations lie within $2.7\%$ of one another. On these dense
two-dimensional datasets the four models are all accurate (their per-region RMSE agrees within $3\%$ except in regions~0 and~1 of
\texttt{combined} and the near-noiseless region~0 of \texttt{hetero\_extreme}, where
absolute errors are below $0.04$), the latent term is small,
and each model's fitted $\sigma_n$ settles on the pooled noise level; this is the
regime in which \cref{eq:var} predicts orthogonality, and \cref{tab:ranksweep} shows it
persists when accuracy differs substantially. A region-aware router chooses, region by
region, among such models. Because each is homoscedastic, it can shift a region's
uncertainty only by the margin by which their global noise estimates differ, a few
percent, against the $50\times$ regional variation that would be needed.

\paragraph{What a router can exploit.} A router gains only where its candidate models
differ in per-region calibration. Measured on held-out data within a seed, that spread
is at most $0.0092$ on three of the four datasets. \texttt{combined}, whose regions
differ in function complexity and sampling density as well as noise, is the exception
(up to $0.0266$ in region~0 and $0.0742$ in region~1), and it is the one constructed
dataset where a non-random router improves on a single model consistently across
seeds (\cref{tab:routing}; on \texttt{varying\_density} only the random-routing
control does by more than $0.0001$): the true-partition oracle by $0.0009$ and
importance routing by $0.0022$ $\ece$, on all three seeds, and the learned oracle by
$0.0021$ on average. These margins are paired, within-seed differences and lie below
the unpaired $\ece$ noise floor of \cref{sec:empirical}. Their source is instructive.
The oracle keeps the exact GP, the uniform choice, in region~1, where the spread is
largest, and gains by switching region~0 to RFF. Region~1's spread is not a difference
in uncertainty at all: every model there predicts $\sigma\approx0.57$ against a true
noise of $0.11$, and the exact GP's lower $\ece$ comes with a \emph{higher} RMSE
($0.227$ against $0.170$ for RFF), its larger errors filling intervals that are too
wide for all four. The spread in calibration that routing can harvest is therefore
small even where it exists, and partly reflects the mean rather than the variance.

\paragraph{Scope.} We test random Fourier features and Nystr\"om/subset-of-regressors
approximations, each with a homoscedastic likelihood, at $n\le4800$. Approximations
whose correction acts as input-dependent noise, notably FITC~\citep{snelson2006} and
PITC~\citep{quinonero2005}, are outside this argument.

\section{Empirical study}
\label{sec:empirical}

\subsection{Protocol}
All methods share one split per (dataset, seed). Exact-GP hyperparameters are fitted
by exact marginal likelihood on a random subsample of at most $1500$ training points,
and sparse models fit their own hyperparameters, including $\sigma_n$, by their own
marginal likelihood on at most $2000$; model and configuration choices are made on a
validation split; the test split is read once. A
single $\ece$ estimator is used for every method, metrics are reported in original
target units, and exact predictive variance is used throughout (\cref{sec:hazard}).

\paragraph{Datasets.} \Cref{tab:data} lists the eleven datasets. Eight are measured,
two are simulators (kin8nm, which we call robot\_arm, and energy), and one,
synthetic\_heteroscedastic, was constructed for the original study. Four further
two-dimensional datasets with known regional structure (\texttt{hetero\_extreme},
\texttt{varying\_smoothness}, \texttt{varying\_density}, \texttt{combined}) are generated
by \texttt{src/structured\_data.py}; their regions are three equal-width bands of $x_1$ (cuts at $\pm1/3$). Noise
changes in steps at the band boundaries, while function complexity ramps smoothly
in $x_1$ and density is concentrated in a Gaussian blob. In the noise-model comparison each dataset is
subsampled to at most $2500$ points and split $60/20/20$, so most datasets train on
$1500$ points but the four smallest train on far fewer. Small test sets bound how
precisely $\ece$ can be measured: for a perfectly calibrated Gaussian our estimator
has expectation $0.015$ at $500$ test points and $0.042$ at $62$
(\texttt{src/data\_checks.py}), so absolute $\ece$ values below these are not
meaningful; paired differences between arms on the same split can resolve less.
Known data issues are documented in \texttt{data/README.md}: SARCOS test inputs also
appear in its training file ($0.6\%$ of test points have an exact training twin at
$n_{\text{train}}=1500$), wine\_white has many duplicate rows ($13\%$ of test points
have a twin), and calhousing targets are censored at $5.0$ ($4.7\%$).

\section{Making the noise model input-dependent}
\label{sec:noise}

\Cref{eq:var} indicates the constructive move: the term that varies across regions is
$\sigma_n^2$, so that is what should be made input-dependent. We compare three arms on
eleven datasets: a single global noise parameter fitted by marginal likelihood; a
two-stage heteroscedastic GP in the spirit of \citet{goldberg1997} and
\citet{kersting2007}, whose noise variance is a $k$NN smoothing of per-point noise
evidence with zero or one refinement pass (re-estimating the evidence under the
first-pass noise); and \emph{regional} noise, a piecewise-constant noise variance over
a partition of the input space by local residual variance. Both input-dependent arms
use the same latent-variance-corrected out-of-fold noise evidence: the squared
out-of-fold residual minus the fold model's latent predictive variance at that point.
Pointwise this is unbiased for $\sigma^2(x_i)$ only if the fold model is well
specified, that is, if its latent variance equals the actual squared error of its mean.
Region means taken over a partition built from the same evidence are biased away from
the overall mean, and are floored at $0.05\,\sigma_n^2$; the floor binds for $0$ to
$59\%$ of test points depending on the dataset (\texttt{src/noise\_controls.py}), and
on yacht the mean evidence is negative in nine of ten seeds, so the floor binds for
most of its test points ($59\%$ on average). Both arms select their configuration on
validation NLL (six configurations for the het-GP, twelve for regional noise) and never
see test labels. Significance is a paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test against
the global arm with Holm correction over the eleven datasets (\texttt{src/significance.py}).

\paragraph{Input-dependent noise pays where there is input-dependence to model.}
Regional noise is the best-calibrated arm on six of eleven datasets. After Holm
correction it is significantly better than global noise on $\ece$ on airfoil, sarcos
and calhousing in seeds $0$--$9$, and on the same three plus
synthetic\_heteroscedastic in the disjoint seeds $10$--$19$; concrete and
synthetic\_heteroscedastic are nominally significant in seeds $0$--$9$ ($p=0.014$,
$0.037$) and all five are Holm-significant when the twenty splits are pooled. It is
significantly worse on $\ece$ on no dataset in any of the three analyses, but on yacht,
the smallest dataset, it is significantly worse on NLL and CRPS (seeds $10$--$19$ and
pooled). The heteroscedastic GP is best on NLL on nine of eleven datasets
and significantly better than global noise on NLL on seven after correction in seeds
$0$--$9$ (nine in seeds $10$--$19$), and significantly worse on yacht.

The datasets with a robust $\ece$ gain have het ratios at least $1.75$ times their
permutation null and train on at least $901$ points. The four with het ratios closest
to their null (protein, robot\_arm, powerplant, wine\_white, at most $1.45$ times) show
no significant difference for regional noise, and there the global arm's $\ece$
already sits near the estimator's noise floor (\cref{sec:empirical}). The two smallest
datasets, yacht and energy ($184$ and $460$ training points), have high het ratios but
show no $\ece$ gain. This pattern was identified after the fact, and across all eleven
datasets neither the het ratio nor its ratio to the null predicts the size of the gain
(Spearman $\rho=0.07$ and $0.09$).

On constructed data the effect is larger. With the true partition, a correctly
specified piecewise-constant noise model and the same out-of-fold evidence, regional
noise improves $\ece$ from $0.2374$ to $0.1367$ on \texttt{hetero\_extreme} and from
$0.0527$ to $0.0285$ on \texttt{combined} (\texttt{src/hetero\_baseline.py}); these are
upper bounds rather than estimates of what a learned partition achieves. It also
improves \texttt{varying\_smoothness} ($0.0675 \to 0.0152$), whose noise is
homoscedastic: there the regional term absorbs model bias that varies with function
complexity, a reminder that input-dependent residual variance is not the same thing as
input-dependent noise.

\paragraph{The earlier, opposite conclusion was an estimator bias.} A previous version
of this analysis found that on $\ece$ global noise was best on nine of eleven datasets
and the het-GP on none. Both input-dependent arms were mis-estimated. The regional arm
used the out-of-fold squared residual as its noise estimate and then added the latent
variance again, counting it twice and making regional noise under-confident, most of
all where latent variance is large, at small $n$. The het-GP smoothed \emph{log}
squared residuals and exponentiated, a geometric mean that for Gaussian residuals sits
at $e^{-1.27}\approx0.28$ of the noise variance. \Cref{sec:process} records this as the
fifth result in this project that turned on how a baseline was configured.

\subsection{Adversarial audit of this section's claim}
\label{sec:adversarial}
We attacked the regional-noise claim six ways: sharpness, granularity and metric
choice on six real datasets with five seeds (\texttt{src/adversarial.py}), global
recalibration and selection on all eleven datasets with ten seeds
(\texttt{src/noise\_controls.py}), and training-set size on the four datasets large
enough for it (\texttt{src/adversarial\_n.py}). None of the attacks overturns it.

\paragraph{Sharpness.} A model with systematically wide intervals can win $\ece$
without being better. Relative to the target's standard deviation, regional intervals
are \emph{narrower} than global ones on five of six datasets, and on four of those
regional noise is simultaneously sharper and better calibrated; the exception to
narrowness is yacht.

\paragraph{Granularity.} Regional noise with $2,3,5,10,20$ regions gives mean $\ece$
between $0.029$ and $0.036$, against $0.048$ for global noise, so the result does not
depend on the number of regions.

\paragraph{Global recalibration.} The input-dependent arms see validation data and the
global arm does not, so we gave the global arm three recalibrations using the same
validation data: a single noise level set to the mean out-of-fold evidence (regional
noise with one region), a noise multiplier chosen from $61$ values in $[0.5, 2]$ on
validation NLL, and a rescaling of the total predictive standard deviation by its
validation-optimal factor. Only the first helps significantly, and only on airfoil
($0.078 \to 0.060$; calhousing $0.069 \to 0.065$, $p=0.19$), while it makes concrete,
sarcos and synthetic\_heteroscedastic significantly worse; neither of the others
improves any dataset significantly. Regional noise remains better than each
recalibration on the same five datasets at uncorrected $p<0.05$, and on airfoil and
calhousing after Holm correction. The global arm's miscalibration is in
the shape of its $z$-distribution, not its scale.

\paragraph{Selection.} Regional noise with one fixed configuration ($3$ regions,
$k=50$) and no selection is still significantly better than global noise on airfoil,
sarcos and calhousing; dropping the noise floor changes no conclusion.

\paragraph{Metric choice.} Regional noise is better than global on $\ece$ at $5$, $10$
and $20$ bins (five, four and four of six datasets), on PIT-KS (five of six), on CRPS
(five of six) and on NLL (four of six). The one metric on which it wins fewer
datasets is coverage error of the $90\%$ interval (two of six), a single point of the
calibration curve.

\paragraph{Training-set size.} A per-region variance estimated from few residuals is
itself noisy, so the comparison could depend on $n$. We sweep $n_{\text{train}}$ from
$300$ to $4000$ with validation and test sets held fixed per seed and nested training
sets (\cref{tab:attackn}). The mean gap favours regional noise at every $n$. It is
large at every $n$ on calhousing and at $n=600$ on airfoil, and within about $1.1$
standard errors of zero at every $n$ on powerplant and wine\_white, the two datasets
with the lowest het ratios.

the oracle-minus-uniform $\ece$ gap remains at zero within noise ($+0.0004$,
$+0.0009$, $-0.0003$, $-0.0004$), and the mean relative spread in predictive standard
deviation across the four fidelities is below $4\%$ above $n=600$ (at most $4.6\%$ for

\subsection{Shrinking regional noise toward global}
\label{sec:shrink}
The natural regulariser for a per-region variance is to shrink it toward the global
one, $\sigma^2_r(\lambda) = \lambda\,\sigma^2_{\text{global}} + (1-\lambda)\,\hat\sigma^2_r$,
with $\lambda$ selected on validation, or an empirical-Bayes
$\lambda_r = (s_r^2/n_r)/(s_r^2/n_r + \tau^2)$ with $\tau^2$ estimated by the method of
moments, which needs no search over $\lambda$.

All three input-dependent arms improve CRPS over global noise by $0.4$--$1.3\%$ at
every $n$ (\cref{tab:shrink}). Shrinkage adds little on top of raw regional noise,
about $0.2\%$ (between $0.02\%$ and $0.33\%$); the paired gain is $2.5$--$3.0$
standard errors at four of the five $n$ ($0.5$ at $n=600$), though nested training
sets make the five $n$ dependent. The selected $\lambda$ shows no trend in $n$
($0.27$--$0.36$), so the variance-reduction story that motivated shrinkage, strong
shrinkage when data is scarce and weak when it is plentiful, is not supported. A
roughly constant $\lambda$ is what one would expect if shrinkage instead corrects the
spread that selection induces in region means built from the same evidence
(\cref{sec:noise}), which depends on $k$ rather than $n$; we have not tested this.

\paragraph{Region identification is not the bottleneck.} Scoring regions by predictive
uncertainty and local sparsity, as the method we audited does, recovers the true
partition only $41$--$49\%$ of the time, against about $33\%$ for uniformly random
labels; on \texttt{varying\_density} and \texttt{combined}, whose true regions are very
unequal in size, agreement mainly reflects label proportions. Scoring by $k$NN-smoothed out-of-fold residual variance raises this
to $96.7\%$ on \texttt{hetero\_extreme}, whose regions differ only in noise. On
\texttt{varying\_smoothness} it reaches $82.0\%$ although the noise is homoscedastic,
because residual variance there tracks model bias. On \texttt{combined} it manages
$44.4\%$ and on \texttt{varying\_density} $36.1\%$, scores that, given the unequal
regions, say little. Residual-variance scoring recovers
the partition when the regions differ in residual variance, whatever its source, and
not otherwise.

\subsection{Seed robustness}
\label{sec:diff}
All results in \cref{tab:noise} use seeds $0$--$9$. On the disjoint seeds $10$--$19$
(\texttt{src/diff\_seeds.py}) regional noise is again nominally significantly better
calibrated than global noise on five datasets (four after Holm correction) and
significantly worse on none, and the het-GP is significantly better on NLL on nine.

Seeds are disjoint splits, not new data. On the four datasets used in full (yacht,
energy, concrete, airfoil) any two splits share about $60\%$ of training and $20\%$ of
test points, so seed-level tests are anti-conservative there. Under a Nadeau--Bengio
corrected resampled $t$-test~\citep{nadeau2003}, the $\ece$ gains of regional noise on
airfoil, sarcos and calhousing remain significant in seeds $0$--$9$ ($p\le0.015$);
those on concrete and synthetic\_heteroscedastic do not ($p=0.18$ and $0.25$;
synthetic\_heteroscedastic reaches $p=0.038$ in seeds $10$--$19$). We regard the first three as
the robust result. In one wine\_white seed in ten the global arm's fitted noise sits at
its lower bound, which inflates that seed's global $\ece$; wine\_white shows no
significant difference either way.

\subsection{Five results that turned on baseline configuration}

In each case a headline number was determined not by the method under study but by how
its comparator was set up. Three were in the work we audited and two were our own;
both of ours would have reversed a conclusion.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Lengthscale in the wrong space.} The median heuristic was computed on
raw $X$ and applied to standardised $X$, wrong by $155{,}000\times$ on protein,
where the baseline degenerated to a constant predictor ($\mathrm{RMSE} = 6.1346$
against $\mathrm{std}(y) = 6.1182$, $R^2 = 0$). The original $+41.1\%$ headline was
measured against these broken baselines; with scale-correct lengthscales, fitted
noise and exact inference, the method without an exact-GP tier is about $-9\%$
against uniform Nystr\"om ($-9.9\%$ and $-9.2\%$ in two runs), and with one between
$+4.5\%$ and $+8.1\%$ (\texttt{results/fair\_exact}; its GPyTorch fits vary between runs).
\item \textbf{Noise never fitted.} The approximations used a hardcoded
$\sigma_n = 0.1$ while the exact GP fitted its noise by marginal likelihood. One arm
was tuned and the others were not.
\item \textbf{Approximate inference in the ``exact'' baseline} (\cref{sec:hazard}),
which perturbed every calibration number above $n=800$ and inflated most, by up to
a factor of ten.
\item \textbf{In-sample residuals.} Our first heteroscedastic-GP comparison estimated
noise from in-sample residuals of a GP conditioned on the same points, which
underestimate it, and the het-GP was worse than global noise on three of four
constructed datasets.
\item \textbf{A biased noise estimator in both input-dependent arms.} Moving to
out-of-fold residuals was not enough. An out-of-fold squared residual is noise plus
latent variance; the regional arm then added the latent variance again, and the
het-GP took a geometric mean of squared residuals, $0.28\times$ too small. Together
these produced a negative result on $\ece$ (global noise best on nine of eleven
datasets, the het-GP on none) and a spurious sample-size reversal that an earlier
draft of this paper reported as a finding. With the estimator corrected, the result
is the opposite (\cref{sec:noise}).
\end{enumerate}

A comparison is only as meaningful as the effort spent on the arm one is not
advocating. Reporting, for every arm, what was fitted, what was selected, on which
split, and what the estimator's expectation is would have caught all five. The fifth
was found only by an independent audit of the code against the paper.

\subsection{A checklist for GP calibration studies}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Confirm the inference path is exact. Raise any Cholesky-size threshold above $n$
and disable fast predictive-variance approximations, for training as well as
prediction; clear the prediction cache between configurations; verify that results
below the threshold are unchanged by the setting.
\item Fit kernel hyperparameters in the space the kernel operates in, and verify a
fitted lengthscale against the median pairwise distance of the \emph{transformed}
inputs.
\item Give every arm the same tuning budget, and state what was fitted and selected
for each.
\item Estimate any residual-derived quantity out-of-fold, and check its expectation:
an out-of-fold squared residual includes latent variance, and the log of a squared
residual is biased by $-1.27$.
\item Screen datasets for the property under study over several seeds, and calibrate
the screening statistic against a permutation null; do not assume the property.
(\cref{tab:screen}: the het ratio's null is $1.8$--$4.1$, not $1$.)
\item Report a proper scoring rule alongside any binned calibration metric, and the
$\ece$ noise floor for the test-set size.
\item Average metrics across datasets only in scale-free form; CRPS, NLL differences
aside, is in target units, and one dataset can dominate a raw mean.
\item When sweeping $n$, hold the validation and test sets fixed.
\item Correct per-dataset significance for the number of datasets, and treat resampled
splits of one dataset as dependent.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item Our constructed datasets reach het ratios of ${\sim}1600$ where measured data
averages at most ${\sim}9$ (\cref{tab:screen}). Two of them vary noise in steps at
equal-width bands of $x_1$ with three regions, which structurally favours a
piecewise-constant noise model and makes the partition easy to recover; they are dense
and two-dimensional, which makes the latent term small and favours orthogonality. The
claims of \cref{sec:noise} rest on the real-data results.
\item Every GP in \cref{sec:orth,sec:empirical,sec:noise} uses an isotropic RBF kernel
(one lengthscale, even for $21$-dimensional sarcos) fitted by L-BFGS-B with at most
$60$ iterations from each of three starts. Misspecification of the mean adds
input-dependent residual variance, which regional noise can absorb; an ARD kernel
might narrow the gap on the datasets where regional noise wins.
\item The heteroscedastic GP is a two-stage $k$NN construction, not a full
implementation of \citet{kersting2007} or of the variational heteroscedastic GP
of~\citet{lazarogredilla2011}. An independent GPyTorch implementation with a GP noise
model (\texttt{src/hetero\_independent.py}, five seeds) agrees where the effect is
large, improving on its own global baseline on airfoil ($0.077 \to 0.048$ $\ece$) and
calhousing ($0.068 \to 0.029$), but is worse on $\ece$ on the other four, most of all
on the two smallest (energy and yacht). How much an input-dependent noise model gains therefore
depends on how it is built; that it gains on the larger heteroscedastic datasets does
not.
\item Residual variance that varies with $x$ may reflect model bias rather than noise
(\cref{sec:noise}, \texttt{varying\_smoothness}); we therefore use the term
\emph{input-dependent residual variance}.
\item Some datasets carry known issues (\cref{sec:empirical}, \texttt{data/README.md}):
duplicated SARCOS test rows, duplicate inputs in wine\_white, censored calhousing
targets, and two simulated datasets. Small test sets limit how finely $\ece$ can
separate arms.
\item Training-set sizes run from $n=184$ to $n=4800$. Behaviour at the $10^5$--$10^6$
scale where sparse approximations are most needed is untested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sparse GP approximation.} Inducing-point and spectral methods
\citep{quinonero2005, titsias2009, rahimi2007, williams2001, smola2000} reduce the
$O(n^3)$ cost by replacing the kernel matrix with a low-rank surrogate. This
literature is overwhelmingly concerned with the \emph{latent} term of~\cref{eq:var};
our observation is that predictive \emph{uncertainty} is usually dominated by the
other term, which is why methods that differ substantially in approximation quality
can be nearly indistinguishable in predictive standard deviation.

\paragraph{Locally adaptive and mixture-of-experts GPs.} The closest prior art
partitions the input space and fits separate models per region
\citep{rasmussen2002, tresp2000, deisenroth2015, gramacy2008}. Our results are not in
tension with it, and the distinction matters. Those methods give each region its own
\emph{kernel and noise hyperparameters}, so per-region predictive variance genuinely
differs; the mechanism we identify, and our noise-model results, are precisely why
that is the right design. What we rule out is the weaker variant (one global model,
with only \emph{approximation fidelity} varying by region), which leaves the dominant
term of \cref{eq:var} untouched. Treed GPs~\citep{gramacy2008} additionally learn the
partition, which our result on residual-variance scoring complements.

\paragraph{Heteroscedastic GPs.} Input-dependent noise has been modelled by a second
GP on log residuals~\citep{goldberg1997}, by EM~\citep{kersting2007}, and
variationally~\citep{lazarogredilla2011}. Our regional noise is a coarse, piecewise
approximation to these, and \cref{sec:noise} finds that even this coarse form
significantly improves calibration on three of our ten real datasets after
correction, four (five of eleven including the constructed one) when twenty splits are
pooled. Our experience adds a practical caution for
two-stage constructions: both the latent variance in out-of-fold residuals and the
log-of-squared-residual bias are large enough to reverse a comparison.

\paragraph{Calibration in regression.} Interval-coverage calibration
\citep{kuleshov2018} and proper scoring rules~\citep{gneiting2007} measure different
things. In our final results they mostly agree (on yacht regional noise is no worse
on $\ece$ but significantly worse on NLL and CRPS in seeds $10$--$19$ and pooled), whereas an earlier, biased version of the comparison had them disagree
systematically, which is one reason we report both.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and measurement validity.} \Cref{sec:hazard} belongs to a
line of work showing that reported gains can originate in evaluation setup rather than
method~\citep{henderson2018, musgrave2020, lipton2019}. Our instance is unusual in
being a silent, threshold-triggered approximation inside a trusted library rather than
a protocol choice, which makes it invisible in code review.

\section{Conclusion}

Region-aware Gaussian processes can adapt two things by region: the fidelity of the
approximation, or the noise model. In our experiments only the second changed the
predictive uncertainty a region receives. The random-feature and Nystr\"om
approximations we tested alter only the latent term of the predictive variance, so
their predictive standard deviations move far less than their accuracy and agree
within $3\%$ in every region of our constructed data, and an oracle router given the
true partition gains nothing on average ($+0.0003$ $\ece$). Making the noise term
regional, by contrast, improves calibration robustly on the larger datasets whose
residual variance is clearly input-dependent (airfoil, sarcos and calhousing under
every correction we apply, five datasets in pooled analyses) and does not worsen
$\ece$ on any, though it worsens NLL and CRPS on the smallest.

The practical readings are that routing approximation fidelity among homoscedastic
approximations is not worth building, that the same regional machinery pointed at the
\emph{noise} model is worth trying when there is enough data and the residual
variance is clearly input-dependent relative to a permutation null (a screen that
separated, after the fact, the datasets that gained from those that did not, but did
not predict the size of the gain), and that the
partition such methods need is recoverable by residual-variance scoring when the
regions differ in residual variance.

Separately, practitioners measuring GP calibration should verify that their inference
path is exact. Above a library default that appears nowhere in user code, ours was
not, and the calibration numbers we computed above it before noticing were inflated
by up to a factor of ten.
