\begin{abstract}
Allocating approximation budget unevenly across the input space --- routing
``important'' regions to high-fidelity approximations and the rest to cheap ones ---
is an appealing way to scale Gaussian processes. We give a quantitative criterion for
when it can work, and show the criterion is rarely satisfied.

The criterion follows from the predictive variance decomposition: sparse
approximations alter only the latent term, while the shared noise term is untouched.
The achievable gain from \emph{any} region-aware router is therefore bounded by the
spread in predictive standard deviation across its candidate models, which is directly
measurable before a router is built. On data whose regional structure is true by
construction, that spread is at most $0.0017$ against a calibration error of order
$0.5$ --- a ratio of $300{:}1$ --- and the criterion correctly predicts both outcomes:
where the spread is negligible an oracle router given the \emph{true} generative
partition gains nothing ($+0.0003$ ECE), and on the one dataset where the spread is
larger ($0.064$) an oracle does gain, by the small margin the spread permits. The
criterion holds from $n=600$ to $n=4800$, and the spread \emph{falls} with $n$.

We contribute three further results. (i) A measurement hazard: an opt-in fast
predictive-variance setting in a widely used GP library is inert below a training-set
size threshold that never appears in user code and above it perturbs predictive
standard deviations by a median of ${\sim}100\%$, inflating measured ECE by
$3$--$9\times$; reported calibration for ``exact'' GPs above this threshold may not
describe the model. (ii) Region identification by local residual variance, which
recovers a known partition $96.9\%$ of the time where uncertainty-and-sparsity scoring
manages $41\%$ against $33\%$ chance. (iii) Shrinkage-regularised regional noise,
whose empirical-Bayes form requires no tuning and is the only arm to improve CRPS at
every sample size tested.

Finally we report the research process (\cref{sec:process}): four of five explanatory
mechanisms we proposed were falsified by their own predictions while claims resting on
direct measurement survived adversarial audit, and four separate headline numbers ---
two in the work we audited, two of our own --- turned on how a \emph{baseline} was
configured. We distil these into a checklist for GP calibration studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Exact Gaussian process regression costs $O(n^3)$, and a large literature reduces this
with sparse approximations \citep{rasmussen2006, quinonero2005, titsias2009}. Most
such methods apply a single approximation uniformly across the input space. It is
natural to ask whether the budget should instead be allocated unevenly: regions that
are sparsely sampled, or where the model is uncertain, might warrant a more faithful
approximation than regions that are dense and easy.

We call this \emph{region-aware approximation}. The premise is appealing and the
mechanism is intuitive. This paper shows the mechanism cannot deliver on the premise,
for a reason that does not depend on the particular partitioning criterion, the
particular approximations, or the quality of the implementation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A measurable criterion} (\cref{sec:orth}). The gain available to any
region-aware router is bounded by the spread in predictive standard deviation across
its candidate models. That spread is computable before a router is built, and it is
small because the shared noise term dominates the predictive variance. The criterion
predicts both where routing fails and where it succeeds.
\item \textbf{A measurement hazard} (\cref{sec:hazard}) that silently corrupts
calibration metrics for ``exact'' GPs above a library default, by $3$--$9\times$.
\item \textbf{Region identification that works} (\cref{sec:noise}): scoring by local
residual variance recovers a known partition $96.9\%$ of the time where the natural
uncertainty-and-sparsity criterion manages $41\%$.
\item \textbf{Shrinkage-regularised regional noise} (\cref{sec:shrink}), whose
empirical-Bayes form needs no tuning and improves CRPS at every sample size tested.
\item \textbf{A record of what failed and what caught it} (\cref{sec:process}),
distilled into a checklist for GP calibration studies.
\end{enumerate}

Where the criterion is not satisfied --- which is the common case --- the consequence
is negative, and we report those consequences in \cref{sec:routing,sec:noise}. We
regard the criterion, not the null results it implies, as the contribution.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Sparse GP approximations.} Given data $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with
$y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_n^2)$, a GP prior
$f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0,k)$ yields a predictive distribution at $x_*$ with variance
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:var}
\mathbb{V}[y_*] \;=\; \underbrace{k(x_*,x_*) - k_*^\top (K+\sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} k_*}_{\text{latent, approximation-dependent}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sigma_n^2}_{\text{shared}} .
\end{equation}
Random Fourier Features \citep{rahimi2007} and Nystr\"om / subset-of-regressors
\citep{williams2001, smola2000} replace the first term with a rank-$m$ surrogate.
Crucially, they leave the second term untouched. \Cref{sec:orth} shows this is what
dooms region-aware approximation.

\paragraph{Calibration metrics.} For regression we use the expected calibration error
over central predictive intervals,
\begin{equation}
\ece = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \Big| \tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_i \mathbf{1}\big[|y_i-\mu_i| \le z_{(1+c_b)/2}\,\sigma_i\big] - c_b \Big|,
\end{equation}
with $c_b$ spanning $[0.1,0.9]$, alongside Gaussian negative log-likelihood. Both
depend on $\sigma_i$, which is what makes \cref{sec:hazard} consequential.

\section{A measurement hazard in approximate predictive variance}
\label{sec:hazard}

Before reporting calibration comparisons we must establish that they measure the
model rather than the inference path.

GPyTorch \citep{gardner2018} provides \texttt{fast\_pred\_var()}, an implementation of
LOVE \citep{pleiss2018} that approximates the predictive covariance, and separately
falls back from Cholesky to iterative solves when the training set exceeds
\texttt{max\_cholesky\_size} (default $800$). We fit one model per cell and vary
\emph{only} the inference path.

A four-way decomposition isolates the cause. Raising \texttt{max\_cholesky\_size}
alone leaves the error unchanged ($101.98\%$ median across cells with $n>800$);
disabling \texttt{fast\_pred\_var()} alone reduces it to $5.00\%$. The threshold
matters only because LOVE is a \emph{no-op} below it: the approximation is silently
inert on small data and silently severe on large data, with no change in user code.

\paragraph{Implication.} Reported $\ece$ or NLL for ``exact'' GPs trained above
$n=800$ in this library may describe an approximation. We recommend that calibration
studies disable fast predictive variance and raise the Cholesky threshold above $n$,
and report having done so.

\section{Fidelity and uncertainty are orthogonal}
\label{sec:orth}

\Cref{eq:var} suggests that if $\sigma_n^2$ dominates, then changing the rank of the
approximation --- which affects only the first term --- barely moves the predictive
standard deviation. We verify this directly on \texttt{hetero\_extreme}, a dataset
whose noise varies $50\times$ across three regions by construction.

On \texttt{hetero\_extreme}, where the regional structure is purely a matter of noise
level, the spread across models within a region is at most $0.0017$ against a
calibration error of order $0.5$ --- a ratio of $300{:}1$. There is nothing to route
between. This is the paper's central mechanism: a router can only choose among the
options it is given, and along the axis that matters --- predictive uncertainty ---
those options are indistinguishable.

\paragraph{The mechanism is predictive, not merely descriptive.} On
\texttt{combined}, whose regions differ in function complexity as well as noise, the
spread is larger: $0.0640$ in region~1, where the exact GP ($0.2860$) genuinely beats
RFF ($0.3500$). The mechanism then predicts that routing should help there, and only
there --- and it does, by exactly the margin the spread permits: \cref{tab:routing}
shows \texttt{combined} is the one constructed dataset where an oracle router improves
on a single model ($0.0525 \to 0.0504$), a gain of $0.0021$. Where models differ,
routing helps in proportion to how much they differ; that difference is small because
the shared noise term dominates \cref{eq:var}. The null result is therefore not an
absence of signal but a quantitative consequence of where predictive variance comes
from.

\section{Empirical study}
\label{sec:empirical}

\subsection{Protocol}
All methods share one split per (dataset, seed); hyperparameters are fitted by
marginal likelihood on training data; model capacity is selected on a validation
split; the test split is read once. A single $\ece$ estimator is used for every
method, and exact predictive variance is used throughout (\cref{sec:hazard}).

\section{Making the noise model regional: when it pays}
\label{sec:noise}

\Cref{eq:var} indicates the constructive move: the term that varies across regions is
$\sigma_n^2$, so that is what should be made regional. On our constructed datasets
this works dramatically, improving $\ece$ by $1.95$--$4.44\times$. It does not survive
contact with real data.

We compare three arms on nine datasets: a single global noise parameter; a
Kersting-style EM heteroscedastic GP \citep{kersting2007}; and per-region noise. All
three estimate noise from shared \emph{out-of-fold} residuals, select hyperparameters
on validation NLL with equal budgets, and never see test labels. Ten seeds.

\paragraph{The method works where the phenomenon is real; the phenomenon is rarely
real.} The two datasets on which regional noise wins are precisely the two whose noise
structure was \emph{constructed} rather than observed. This is a useful consistency
check --- the machinery does what it is designed to do --- and simultaneously the
result's undoing: across nine real datasets spanning het ratios of $2.2$ to $7.1$,
there was never enough input-dependent noise to repay the parameters spent modelling
it. Note also that a dataset's het ratio does not predict which arm wins: energy, the
most heteroscedastic real dataset at $7.05$, is a clean win for global noise.

On NLL the ordering partly reverses --- regional wins seven of eleven --- which says
the regional model sharpens predictions without making their intervals better
calibrated. On $\ece$, \textbf{at this training-set size}, neither input-dependent
noise model improves calibration over a single global noise parameter. The
qualification is essential: \cref{sec:adversarial} shows the result reverses by
$n=4000$. The
gains we observed on constructed data appear only at het ratios of order $10^3$, some
$200\times$ beyond anything we measured in practice (\cref{tab:screen}). At realistic
levels the additional parameters cost more in estimation variance than they recover in
bias, and both noise models can be misled by residual variance that reflects model
bias rather than noise.

\subsection{Adversarial audit of this section's claim}
\label{sec:adversarial}
We attacked the claim of this section five ways (\texttt{src/adversarial.py},
\texttt{src/adversarial\_n.py}). Three attacks failed and two succeeded; we report
both.

\paragraph{Failed: sharpness.} A model with systematically wide intervals can win
$\ece$ without being better. Mean predictive widths are global $1.52$, regional
$1.72$: regional is the \emph{wider} arm and still loses on calibration, so global is
simultaneously sharper and better calibrated.

\paragraph{Failed: granularity.} Regional noise with $2,3,5,10,20$ regions improves
monotonically ($\ece$ $0.0960 \to 0.0806$) but never approaches global ($0.0477$),
even as the partition approaches a smooth noise model.

\paragraph{Failed: tuning parity.} Giving the global arm the same
validation-selected freedom as the others changes little.

\paragraph{Succeeded: metric choice.} On calibration metrics ($\ece$ at $5/10/20$
bins, $90\%$ coverage error) global wins on most datasets. On the two \emph{proper}
scoring rules the ranking reverses by dataset count: regional wins CRPS on four of six
and NLL on four of six, though global remains better on the mean of every metric. The
split is systematic, not noise: regional noise buys sharpness at the cost of
calibration.

\paragraph{Succeeded, and decisively: training-set size.} The gap is a function of
$n$, and it reverses.

\paragraph{Consequence.} \textbf{The negative result for regional noise is an artifact
of training-set size and must be withdrawn as a general claim.} Our main experiments
cap $n_{\text{train}}$ at ${\sim}1500$, where regional noise does lose; by $n=4000$ it
wins. The mechanism is unsurprising in hindsight --- a per-region variance estimated
from a few hundred residuals is itself high-variance --- but it was not visible until
we swept $n$ deliberately. The correct statement is that \emph{regional noise requires
more data than we gave it}, and the crossover on these datasets is near $n\approx2500$.

This does \emph{not} affect \cref{sec:orth,sec:routing}. The orthogonality result
concerns the decomposition in \cref{eq:var} and the observation that models of
differing fidelity emit near-identical predictive variance; neither depends on
estimating a noise level from limited residuals. We checked this directly rather than
relying on the argument (\texttt{src/routing\_largen.py}), since assuming a result is
$n$-independent is precisely the error attack~E exposed. Sweeping $n_{\text{train}}$
from $600$ to $4800$, the oracle-minus-uniform $\ece$ gap remains at zero within
noise ($+0.0004$, $+0.0009$, $-0.0003$, $-0.0004$), and the relative spread in mean
predictive standard deviation across the four fidelities \emph{falls} with $n$
($32.4\%$ at $n{=}600$, where the approximations are over-parameterised relative to
the data, then $1.5\%$, $2.4\%$, $3.6\%$). Routing headroom does not appear at scale,
and orthogonality strengthens.

\subsection{Repairing regional noise by shrinkage}
\label{sec:shrink}
Attack~E diagnoses regional noise as a bias--variance problem: a per-region variance
estimated from a few hundred residuals is itself high-variance. The standard remedy is
to shrink the regional estimate toward the global one,
$\sigma^2_r(\lambda) = \lambda\,\sigma^2_{\text{global}} + (1-\lambda)\,\hat\sigma^2_r$,
with $\lambda$ selected on validation. We also test an empirical-Bayes variant
requiring no search, $\lambda_r = (s_r^2/n_r)/(s_r^2/n_r + \tau^2)$, in which regions
with fewer points shrink harder automatically.

datasets and three seeds. Shrinkage or empirical Bayes is best at every $n$; no other
arm is.}

 600 & 1.1497 & 1.1435 & 1.1433 & \textbf{1.1409} & EB \\

The gains are small (${\sim}0.3\%$) but they are the only ones that hold at every
sample size, and the empirical-Bayes variant obtains them without a tuning knob.

\paragraph{Two honest limits.} First, the improvement is confined to proper scoring
rules. On $\ece$ shrinkage can be \emph{worse} than the global arm it interpolates
toward --- $0.0714$ versus $0.0438$ at $n{=}300$ --- because $\lambda$ is selected on
validation NLL while $\ece$ would prefer $\lambda\approx1$. This is the
sharpness--calibration tradeoff of \cref{sec:adversarial} reappearing as a
criterion--metric mismatch.

Second, the mechanism we proposed is not supported. If shrinkage worked because
per-region estimates are noisy at small $n$, the selected $\lambda$ should fall as $n$
grows. It does not: the selected values are $0.27, 0.10, 0.18, 0.16, 0.22$ across
$n = 300 \ldots 4000$, with no trend. The method helps; our explanation for why does
not survive its own prediction, and we do not offer a replacement without evidence.

\paragraph{Region identification is not the bottleneck.} It is worth ruling out the
obvious rescue. Scoring regions by predictive uncertainty and local sparsity --- the
natural choice --- recovers the true partition only $41$--$49\%$ of the time against
$33\%$ chance. Scoring by $k$NN-smoothed residual variance raises this to $96.9\%$ and
$81.9\%$ where noise regions exist, and correctly stays at chance where density varies
but noise does not. Region identification is therefore solvable; solving it does not
make region-aware modelling pay.

\subsection{Seed robustness}
\label{sec:diff}
All reported results use seeds $0$--$9$. We re-ran the identical protocol on the
disjoint seeds $10$--$19$ (\texttt{src/diff\_seeds.py}). Per-dataset winners agree on
ten of eleven datasets; the $\ece$ best-arm count moves from global~$9$~/~regional~$2$
to global~$8$~/~regional~$3$, the single flip being powerplant, whose margin is small
($0.0197$ vs $0.0218$). The heteroscedastic GP wins zero datasets on $\ece$ in both
seed sets. The conclusion does not depend on the seeds.

\section{Process: which claims failed, and what caught them}
\label{sec:process}

This project began as an attempt to \emph{demonstrate} region-aware approximation and
became a negative result only after a sequence of claims --- most of them our own ---
failed under test. We report that sequence, because the failure modes recur and
because a reader deciding how much to trust \cref{sec:orth,sec:routing} should know
which parts of this work were checked adversarially and which were merely not yet
falsified.

\subsection{Four results that turned on baseline configuration}

In each case a headline number was determined not by the method under study but by how
its comparator was set up. Twice this favoured the proposed method; twice it favoured
ours, once with the sign reversed.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Lengthscale in the wrong space.} The median heuristic was computed on
raw $X$ and applied to standardised $X$ --- wrong by $155{,}000\times$ on protein,
where the baseline degenerated to a constant predictor ($\mathrm{RMSE} = 6.1346$
against $\mathrm{std}(y) = 6.1182$, $R^2 = 0$). The original $+41.1\%$ headline was
the distance to that model; corrected, it is $-9.9\%$.
\item \textbf{Noise never fitted.} The approximations used a hardcoded
$\sigma_n = 0.1$ while the exact GP fitted its noise by marginal likelihood. One arm
was tuned and the others were not.
\item \textbf{Approximate inference in the ``exact'' baseline} (\cref{sec:hazard}),
which corrupted every calibration number above $n=800$ by $3$--$9\times$.
\item \textbf{In-sample residuals, handicapping \emph{our} baseline.} Our first
heteroscedastic-GP comparison estimated noise from in-sample residuals, which
underestimate by $1.1$--$1.9\times$. Regional noise averages over groups so the bias
cancels; the het-GP uses them pointwise and compounds them through EM. The het-GP lost
on all nine datasets, and we came close to reporting it.
\end{enumerate}

The recurring shape is that a comparison is only as meaningful as the effort spent on
the arm one is not advocating. We suggest reporting, for every arm, what was fitted,
what was selected, and on which split --- a discipline that would have caught all four.

\subsection{Verifications that passed without running}

Three checks reported success while failing:

\begin{itemize}
\item An audit script printed \texttt{CLEAN: no selection on test labels} three times.
Under \texttt{zsh} an unquoted array variable reaches \texttt{grep} as a single
argument; the greps errored and a \texttt{|| echo} fallback reported the pass.
\item A crashing script reported \texttt{EXIT=0}, because a pipeline's exit status is
its last stage and the traceback had been piped to \texttt{tail}.
\item \Cref{tab:orth}, the paper's central mechanism, was generated by an ad-hoc
command that was never committed. Regenerating it from a script changed the numbers:
the effect was stronger than reported on one dataset and the general claim as
originally stated was wrong (\cref{sec:orth}).
\end{itemize}

A verification that cannot fail loudly is not a verification. We now require every
reported number to be produced by a committed script reachable from
\texttt{reproduce.sh}, which covers all $19$ scripts behind this paper.

\subsection{A checklist for GP calibration studies}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Confirm the inference path is exact. Disable fast predictive-variance
approximations and raise any Cholesky-size threshold above $n$; verify that results
below the threshold are unchanged by the setting.
\item Fit kernel hyperparameters in the space the kernel operates in, and verify a
fitted lengthscale against the median pairwise distance of the \emph{transformed}
inputs.
\item Give every arm the same tuning budget, and state what was fitted and selected
for each.
\item Estimate any residual-derived quantity out-of-fold.
\item Screen datasets for the property under study; do not assume it. A dataset named
for a phenomenon may not exhibit it --- \texttt{synthetic\_heteroscedastic} here ranks
below an ordinary robotics benchmark (\cref{tab:screen}).
\item Report a proper scoring rule alongside any binned calibration metric; the two
can disagree systematically (\cref{sec:adversarial}).
\item Sweep $n$ before concluding anything about a method that estimates per-region
quantities. Our own negative result reversed at $n=4000$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item Our constructed datasets reach het ratios of ${\sim}1557$ where real data reaches
at most ${\sim}7$ (\cref{tab:screen}), and vary noise in steps rather than smoothly,
which structurally favours a piecewise-constant noise model. Claims in
\cref{sec:noise} rest on the real-data results, not the constructed ones.
\item Our heteroscedastic baseline is re-run against an independent GPyTorch
implementation (\texttt{src/hetero\_independent.py}) --- different linear algebra,
optimiser and codebase. That tests the implementation, not the algorithm family: both
are two-stage Goldberg-style constructions. A structurally different heteroscedastic
GP, such as the variational model of \citet{lazarogredilla2011}, remains untested.
\item Residual variance that varies with $x$ may reflect model bias rather than noise;
we therefore use the term \emph{input-dependent residual variance}.
\item Training-set sizes run from $n=300$ to $n=4800$. The criterion of
\cref{sec:orth} was verified across that whole range and the model spread falls with
$n$, but behaviour at the $10^5$--$10^6$ scale where sparse approximations are most
needed is untested, and we would not extrapolate the noise-model results there given
that one of them already reversed within the range we did test.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sparse GP approximation.} Inducing-point and spectral methods
\citep{quinonero2005, titsias2009, rahimi2007, williams2001, smola2000} reduce the
$O(n^3)$ cost by replacing the kernel matrix with a low-rank surrogate. This
literature is overwhelmingly concerned with the \emph{latent} term of \cref{eq:var};
our observation is that predictive \emph{uncertainty} is usually dominated by the
other term, which is why methods that differ substantially in approximation quality
can be nearly indistinguishable in predictive standard deviation.

\paragraph{Locally adaptive and mixture-of-experts GPs.} The closest prior art
partitions the input space and fits separate models per region
\citep{rasmussen2002, tresp2000, deisenroth2015, gramacy2008}. Our results are not in
tension with it, and the distinction matters. Those methods give each region its own
\emph{kernel and noise hyperparameters}, so per-region predictive variance genuinely
differs; the mechanism we identify is precisely why that is the right design. What we
rule out is the weaker variant --- holding one global model and varying only
\emph{approximation fidelity} by region --- which leaves the dominant term of
\cref{eq:var} untouched. Treed GPs \citep{gramacy2008} additionally learn the
partition, which our \cref{sec:noise} result on residual-variance scoring complements:
we find the partition is recoverable when scored by the right quantity.

\paragraph{Heteroscedastic GPs.} Input-dependent noise has been modelled by a second
GP on log residuals \citep{goldberg1997}, by EM \citep{kersting2007}, and
variationally \citep{lazarogredilla2011}. Our regional noise is a coarse, piecewise
approximation to these, and \cref{sec:shrink} finds its value is modest and confined
to proper scoring rules. \Cref{sec:adversarial} contributes a practical caveat for
this literature: the advantage of any input-dependent noise model over a single
parameter is strongly $n$-dependent, and on our datasets reverses sign near
$n \approx 2500$, so comparisons at a single training-set size can reach opposite
conclusions.

\paragraph{Calibration in regression.} Interval-coverage calibration
\citep{kuleshov2018} and proper scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007} measure different
things, and \cref{sec:adversarial} shows they rank our methods differently in a
systematic way: regional noise buys sharpness at the cost of coverage. We therefore
report both throughout, and recommend the practice.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and measurement validity.} \Cref{sec:hazard} belongs to a
line of work showing that reported gains can originate in evaluation setup rather than
method \citep{henderson2018, musgrave2020, lipton2019}. Our instance is unusual in
being a silent, threshold-triggered approximation inside a trusted library rather than
a protocol choice, which makes it invisible in code review.

\section{Conclusion}

The gain available to a region-aware Gaussian process is bounded by how much its
candidate models differ in predictive standard deviation, and that quantity is
measurable in advance. Because sparse approximations alter only the latent term of the
predictive variance while the shared noise term is untouched, the bound is usually
tight and small: on our data the spread across four fidelities is $0.0017$ against a
calibration error of $0.5$. The criterion is predictive in both directions --- it
identifies the one dataset where an oracle router does gain, and the size of that gain
--- and it holds from $n=600$ to $n=4800$, tightening as $n$ grows.

The practical readings are that routing approximation fidelity by region is not worth
building, that the same regional machinery pointed at the \emph{noise} model is worth
a little and is best regularised by shrinkage, and that the partition such methods
need is recoverable provided it is scored by residual variance rather than by
uncertainty and sparsity.

Separately, practitioners measuring GP calibration should verify that their inference
path is exact. Above a library default that appears nowhere in user code, ours was
not, and every calibration number we computed before noticing was wrong by
$3$--$9\times$.
