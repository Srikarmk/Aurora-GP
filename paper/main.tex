\begin{abstract}
Region-aware Gaussian processes adapt the model to different parts of the input
space. One appealing form allocates approximation budget unevenly, routing
``important'' regions to high-fidelity sparse approximations and the rest to cheap
ones. We show this form cannot change the uncertainty a region receives, and that a
second form, adapting the noise model by region, can.

The reason is the predictive variance decomposition: sparse approximations alter only
the latent term, while the noise term is shared. On four constructed datasets with
known regional structure, models spanning a wide range of fidelity emit mean
predictive standard deviations within $2\%$ of one another in every region, while
the true noise varies $50\times$ across regions. An oracle router given the true
generative partition gains nothing ($+0.0003$ ECE), and on five standard benchmarks
importance routing is significantly worse than a random-routing control on two. Making the noise term
input-dependent instead, with an unbiased out-of-fold noise estimate, pays: regional
noise improves calibration significantly on five of eleven datasets and worsens it on
none, and a heteroscedastic GP improves NLL on eight. The gains appear where measured
residual variance is input-dependent and the training set is not tiny; the result
replicates on disjoint seeds and survives five adversarial checks, including a sweep
over training-set size.

We also report a measurement hazard: an opt-in fast predictive-variance setting in a
widely used GP library is inert below a training-set size threshold that never
appears in user code, and above it perturbs predictive standard deviations by a
median of ${\sim}100\%$, inflating measured ECE by a factor of three to ten at
$n=4000$. Finally we report the research process (\cref{sec:process}): five of six
mechanisms we proposed did not survive, and five headline numbers, including an
earlier, opposite conclusion of this paper, turned on how a \emph{baseline} or
estimator was configured. We distil these into a checklist for GP calibration studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Exact Gaussian process regression costs $O(n^3)$, and a large literature reduces this
with sparse approximations~\citep{rasmussen2006, quinonero2005, titsias2009}. Most
such methods apply a single approximation uniformly across the input space. It is
natural to ask whether the budget should instead be allocated unevenly: regions that
are sparsely sampled, or where the model is uncertain, might warrant a more faithful
approximation than regions that are dense and easy.

We call this \emph{region-aware approximation}. The premise is appealing and the
mechanism is intuitive. This paper shows the mechanism cannot deliver on the premise,
for a reason that does not depend on the particular partitioning criterion, the
particular approximations, or the quality of the implementation: approximation
fidelity and predictive uncertainty are nearly orthogonal. The same reason points to
what does work. The term of the predictive variance that can differ by region is the
noise, and adapting it by region improves calibration where the data warrant it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fidelity and uncertainty are orthogonal} (\cref{sec:orth,sec:routing}).
Models of very different approximation quality emit predictive standard deviations
within $2\%$ of one another in every region of our four constructed datasets, so routing among them cannot
change a region's uncertainty; oracle routing gains nothing, and the importance
criterion of the method we audited is significantly worse than random routing on two
of five benchmarks.
\item \textbf{Input-dependent noise pays where a screen says it should}
(\cref{sec:noise}). With an unbiased out-of-fold noise estimate, regional noise and a
regional noise beats a single global noise parameter on $\ece$ wherever measured
residual variance is input-dependent and there is enough data to estimate it, robustly
across seeds, metrics, region counts and training-set sizes; a two-stage
heteroscedastic GP improves NLL on eight of eleven datasets.
\item \textbf{A measurement hazard} (\cref{sec:hazard}) that silently corrupts
calibration metrics for ``exact'' GPs above a library default, by up to a factor of ten.
\item \textbf{A record of what failed and what caught it} (\cref{sec:process}),
including a reversal of this paper's own earlier conclusion, distilled into a
checklist for GP calibration studies.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Sparse GP approximations.} Given data $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with
$y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_n^2)$, a GP prior
$f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0,k)$ yields a predictive distribution at $x_*$ with variance
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:var}
\mathbb{V}[y_*] \;=\; \underbrace{k(x_*,x_*) - k_*^\top (K+\sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} k_*}_{\text{latent, approximation-dependent}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sigma_n^2}_{\text{shared}}.
\end{equation}
Random Fourier Features~\citep{rahimi2007} and Nystr\"om / subset-of-regressors
\citep{williams2001, smola2000} replace the first term with a rank-$m$ surrogate.
Crucially, they leave the second term untouched. \Cref{sec:orth} shows this is what
dooms region-aware approximation.

\paragraph{Calibration metrics.} For regression we use the expected calibration error
over central predictive intervals,
\begin{equation}
\ece = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \Big| \tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_i \mathbf{1}\big[|y_i-\mu_i| \le z_{(1+c_b)/2}\,\sigma_i\big] - c_b \Big|,
\end{equation}
with $c_b$ spanning $[0.1,0.9]$, alongside Gaussian negative log-likelihood. Both
depend on $\sigma_i$, which is what makes~\cref{sec:hazard} consequential.

\section{A measurement hazard in approximate predictive variance}
\label{sec:hazard}

Before reporting calibration comparisons we must establish that they measure the
model rather than the inference path.

GPyTorch~\citep{gardner2018} provides \texttt{fast\_pred\_var()}, an implementation of
LOVE~\citep{pleiss2018} that approximates the predictive covariance, and separately
falls back from Cholesky to iterative solves when the training set exceeds
\texttt{max\_cholesky\_size} (default $800$). We fit one model per cell and vary
\emph{only} the inference path. All measurements use GPyTorch 1.15.2 with
linear\_operator 0.6.1.

\Cref{fig:corruption} shows the onset is sharp rather than gradual. A four-way
decomposition isolates the cause. Raising \texttt{max\_cholesky\_size}
alone leaves the error unchanged ($101.98\%$ median across cells with $n>800$);
disabling \texttt{fast\_pred\_var()} alone reduces it to $5.00\%$. The threshold
matters because LOVE is a \emph{no-op} below it: the approximation is silently inert
on small data and silently severe on large data, with no change in user code. The
iterative-solve fallback is not exact either: with LOVE off it still perturbs $\sigma$
by up to $38\%$ (sarcos, $n=1000$), so both settings must be changed.

\paragraph{Implication.} Reported $\ece$ or NLL for ``exact'' GPs trained above
$n=800$ in this library may describe an approximation. We recommend that calibration
studies disable fast predictive variance and raise the Cholesky threshold above $n$,
and report having done so.

\section{Fidelity and uncertainty are orthogonal}
\label{sec:orth}

\Cref{eq:var} suggests that if $\sigma_n^2$ dominates, then changing the rank of the
approximation, which affects only the first term, barely moves the predictive
standard deviation. We verify this directly on four constructed datasets with known
regional structure, including \texttt{hetero\_extreme}, whose noise standard deviation
varies $50\times$ across three regions by construction. Each model fits its own
hyperparameters, including $\sigma_n$, by its own marginal likelihood.

\label{tab:orth}
\small\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\input{tables/orthogonality.tex}
\end{table}

Across all four datasets and all twelve regions, models whose approximation quality
differs widely emit mean predictive standard deviations within $1.9\%$ of one another
($0.37\%$ on \texttt{hetero\_extreme}). A region-aware router chooses, region by region,
among these models. Along the axis that matters for calibration, predictive
uncertainty, they are nearly indistinguishable, so routing cannot give a region the
uncertainty it needs. This is the paper's central mechanism, and it follows from where
predictive variance comes from rather than from any particular router.

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/orthogonality.pdf}
\caption{Per-region ECE for four models spanning a wide range of approximation
fidelity; bars are the range over three seeds. On \texttt{hetero\_extreme} (left) the
four models are indistinguishable in every region. On \texttt{combined} (right) they
separate in regions 0 and 1, the only place in our constructed data where a router has
anything to choose between.}
\label{fig:orth}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What a router can exploit.} A router gains only where its candidate models
differ in per-region calibration, which can be measured on validation data before a
router is built. That spread is at most $0.0036$ on three of the four datasets.
\texttt{combined}, whose regions differ in function complexity and sampling density as
well as noise, is the exception ($0.0215$ in region~0, $0.0640$ in region~1), and it is
the one constructed dataset where a non-random router improves on a single model by a
resolvable margin (\cref{tab:routing}; on \texttt{varying\_density} only the
random-routing control does, by $0.0005$): the true-partition oracle by $0.0009$ and importance routing by
$0.0022$ $\ece$, on all three seeds, and the learned oracle by $0.0021$ on average.
The gain is small, and its source is instructive. The oracle keeps the exact GP, the
uniform choice, in region~1, where the spread is largest, and gains by switching
region~0 to RFF. Region~1's spread is not a difference in uncertainty at all: every
model there predicts $\sigma\approx0.57$ against a true noise of $0.11$, and the exact
GP's lower $\ece$ comes with a \emph{higher} RMSE ($0.227$ against $0.170$ for RFF),
its larger errors filling intervals that are too wide for all four. The spread in
calibration that routing can harvest is therefore small even where it exists, and
partly reflects the mean rather than the variance.

\section{Empirical study}
\label{sec:empirical}

\subsection{Protocol}
All methods share one split per (dataset, seed). Exact-GP hyperparameters are fitted
by exact marginal likelihood on a random subsample of at most $1500$ training points,
and sparse models fit their own hyperparameters, including $\sigma_n$, by their own
marginal likelihood on at most $2000$; model and configuration choices are made on a
validation split; the test split is read once. A
single $\ece$ estimator is used for every method, metrics are reported in original
target units, and exact predictive variance is used throughout (\cref{sec:hazard}).

\paragraph{Datasets.} \Cref{tab:data} lists the eleven datasets. Eight are measured,
two are simulators (kin8nm, which we call robot\_arm, and energy), and one,
synthetic\_heteroscedastic, was constructed for the original study. Four further
two-dimensional datasets with known regional structure (\texttt{hetero\_extreme},
\texttt{varying\_smoothness}, \texttt{varying\_density}, \texttt{combined}) are generated
by \texttt{src/structured\_data.py}; their regions are the tertiles of $x_1$. Noise
changes in steps at the tertile boundaries, while function complexity ramps smoothly
in $x_1$ and density is concentrated in a Gaussian blob. In the noise-model comparison each dataset is
subsampled to at most $2500$ points and split $60/20/20$, so most datasets train on
$1500$ points but the four smallest train on far fewer. Small test sets bound how
precisely $\ece$ can be measured: for a perfectly calibrated Gaussian our estimator
has expectation $0.015$ at $500$ test points and $0.042$ at $62$, so differences
below about $0.01$ on the larger datasets, and $0.03$ on yacht, are not resolvable.
Known data issues are documented in \texttt{data/README.md}: SARCOS test inputs also
appear in its training file ($0.6\%$ of test points have an exact training twin at
$n_{\text{train}}=1500$), wine\_white has many duplicate rows ($13\%$ of test points
have a twin), and calhousing targets are censored at $5.0$ ($4.7\%$).

\section{Making the noise model input-dependent}
\label{sec:noise}

\Cref{eq:var} indicates the constructive move: the term that varies across regions is
$\sigma_n^2$, so that is what should be made input-dependent. We compare three arms on
eleven datasets: a single global noise parameter fitted by marginal likelihood; a
two-stage heteroscedastic GP in the spirit of \citet{goldberg1997} and
\citet{kersting2007}, whose noise variance is a $k$NN smoothing of per-point noise
evidence with zero or one refinement pass (re-estimating the evidence under the
first-pass noise); and \emph{regional} noise, a
piecewise-constant noise variance over a partition of the input space by local
residual variance. Both input-dependent arms use the same out-of-fold noise evidence,
the squared out-of-fold residual minus the fold model's latent predictive variance at
that point, which is unbiased for $\sigma^2(x_i)$ when the fold model's latent
variance matches its actual error, that is, up to model misspecification such as
squared bias of the mean.
Both select their configuration on validation NLL (six configurations for the het-GP,
twelve for regional noise) and never see test labels. Ten seeds; significance is a
paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test against the global arm.

\paragraph{Input-dependent noise pays where there is input-dependence to model.} Regional noise
is the best-calibrated arm on six of eleven datasets, significantly better than a
single global noise parameter on five (sarcos, airfoil, calhousing, concrete,
synthetic\_heteroscedastic) and significantly worse on none. The heteroscedastic GP is
best on NLL on nine of eleven and on CRPS on nine, significantly better than global
noise on NLL on eight. All five datasets with a significant $\ece$ gain have mean het
ratios of at least $4.0$ and train on at least $618$ points. The four with the lowest
het ratios (protein, robot\_arm, powerplant, wine\_white, all below $2.7$) show no
significant $\ece$ difference for regional noise, and there the global arm's $\ece$
already sits at the estimator's noise floor (\cref{sec:empirical}). The two datasets
with the highest het ratios, yacht and energy, are also the two smallest ($184$ and
$460$ training points), and regional noise does not separate from global noise on
$\ece$ on either. The het-GP is significantly worse on $\ece$ on yacht and on protein;
regional noise is significantly worse on none (on CRPS it is significantly worse on
yacht).

On constructed data the effect is larger. With the true partition and the same
out-of-fold evidence, regional noise improves $\ece$ from $0.2374$ to $0.1367$ on
\texttt{hetero\_extreme} and from $0.0527$ to $0.0285$ on \texttt{combined}
(\texttt{src/hetero\_baseline.py}). It also improves \texttt{varying\_smoothness}
($0.0675 \to 0.0152$), whose noise is homoscedastic: there the regional term absorbs
model bias that varies with function complexity, a reminder that input-dependent
residual variance is not the same thing as input-dependent noise.

\paragraph{The earlier, opposite conclusion was an estimator bias.} A previous version
of this analysis found that global noise won on nine of eleven datasets and that the
het-GP won on none. Both input-dependent arms were mis-estimated. The regional arm
used the out-of-fold squared residual as its noise estimate and then added the latent
variance again, counting it twice and making regional noise under-confident, most of
all where latent variance is large, at small $n$. The het-GP smoothed \emph{log}
squared residuals and exponentiated, a geometric mean that for Gaussian residuals sits
at $e^{-1.27}\approx0.28$ of the noise variance. \Cref{sec:process} records this as the
fifth result in this project that turned on how a baseline was configured.

\subsection{Adversarial audit of this section's claim}
\label{sec:adversarial}
We attacked the regional-noise claim five ways with five seeds, four of them on six
real datasets and the sample-size sweep on the four large enough for it
(\texttt{src/adversarial.py}, \texttt{src/adversarial\_n.py}). None of the attacks
overturns it.

\paragraph{Sharpness.} A model with systematically wide intervals can win $\ece$
without being better. Relative to the target's standard deviation, regional intervals
are \emph{narrower} than global ones on five of six datasets, and on four of those
regional noise is simultaneously sharper and better calibrated; the exception to
narrowness is yacht.

\paragraph{Granularity.} Regional noise with $2,3,5,10,20$ regions gives mean $\ece$
between $0.029$ and $0.036$, against $0.048$ for global noise, so the result does not
depend on the number of regions.

\paragraph{Tuning parity.} Giving the global arm a validation-selected noise multiplier
changes it in six of thirty cells, and on no dataset does it lift the global arm above regional noise.

\paragraph{Metric choice.} Regional noise is better than global on $\ece$ at $5$, $10$
and $20$ bins (five, four and four of six datasets), on PIT-KS (five of six), on CRPS
(five of six) and on NLL (four of six). The one metric on which it wins fewer
datasets is coverage error of the $90\%$ interval (two of six), a single point of the
calibration curve.

\paragraph{Training-set size.} A per-region variance estimated from few residuals is
itself noisy, so the comparison could depend on $n$. We sweep $n_{\text{train}}$ from
$300$ to $4000$ with validation and test sets held fixed per seed and nested training
sets (\cref{tab:attackn}). The mean gap favours regional noise at every $n$. It is
large at every $n$ on calhousing and at $n=600$ on airfoil, and within about $1.1$
standard errors of zero at every $n$ on powerplant and wine\_white, the two datasets
with the lowest het ratios.

\paragraph{Routing approximation fidelity at larger $n$.} The sample-size question
applies equally to \cref{sec:orth,sec:routing}, so we checked it there too
(\texttt{src/routing\_largen.py}). Sweeping $n_{\text{train}}$ from $600$ to $4800$,
the oracle-minus-uniform $\ece$ gap remains at zero within noise ($+0.0004$,
$+0.0009$, $-0.0003$, $-0.0004$), and the mean relative spread in predictive standard
deviation across the four fidelities is below $4\%$ above $n=600$ (at most $4.6\%$ for
any single dataset; $n=4800$ covers calhousing and powerplant only). At $n=600$ it is
$32\%$, driven by wine\_white at $93\%$, where the sparse models are fitted on very
little data. Routing headroom
does not appear within this range.

\subsection{Shrinking regional noise toward global}
\label{sec:shrink}
The natural regulariser for a per-region variance is to shrink it toward the global
one, $\sigma^2_r(\lambda) = \lambda\,\sigma^2_{\text{global}} + (1-\lambda)\,\hat\sigma^2_r$,
with $\lambda$ selected on validation, or an empirical-Bayes
$\lambda_r = (s_r^2/n_r)/(s_r^2/n_r + \tau^2)$ with $\tau^2$ estimated by the method of
moments, which needs no search over $\lambda$.

All three input-dependent arms improve CRPS over global noise by $0.4$--$1.3\%$ at

\paragraph{Region identification is not the bottleneck.} Scoring regions by predictive
uncertainty and local sparsity, as the method we audited does, recovers the true
partition only $41$--$49\%$ of the time, against $33$--$34\%$ for random labels. Scoring by $k$NN-smoothed out-of-fold residual variance raises this
to $96.7\%$ on \texttt{hetero\_extreme}, whose regions differ only in noise. On
\texttt{varying\_smoothness} it reaches $82.0\%$ although the noise is homoscedastic,
because residual variance there tracks model bias. On \texttt{combined} it manages
$44.4\%$ and on \texttt{varying\_density} $36.1\%$, both below the
uncertainty-and-sparsity score ($49.0\%$, $45.2\%$). Residual-variance scoring recovers
the partition when the regions differ in residual variance, whatever its source, and
not otherwise.

\subsection{Seed robustness}
\label{sec:diff}
All results in \cref{tab:noise} use seeds $0$--$9$. On the disjoint seeds $10$--$19$
(\texttt{src/diff\_seeds.py}) regional noise is again significantly better calibrated
than global noise on five datasets and significantly worse on none, and the het-GP is
significantly better on NLL on nine. The conclusion does not depend on the seeds.

\subsection{Five results that turned on baseline configuration}

In each case a headline number was determined not by the method under study but by how
its comparator was set up. Three were in the work we audited and two were our own;
both of ours would have reversed a conclusion.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Lengthscale in the wrong space.} The median heuristic was computed on
raw $X$ and applied to standardised $X$, wrong by $155{,}000\times$ on protein,
where the baseline degenerated to a constant predictor ($\mathrm{RMSE} = 6.1346$
against $\mathrm{std}(y) = 6.1182$, $R^2 = 0$). The original $+41.1\%$ headline was
the distance to that model; corrected, it is $-9.9\%$.
\item \textbf{Noise never fitted.} The approximations used a hardcoded
$\sigma_n = 0.1$ while the exact GP fitted its noise by marginal likelihood. One arm
was tuned and the others were not.
\item \textbf{Approximate inference in the ``exact'' baseline} (\cref{sec:hazard}),
which perturbed every calibration number above $n=800$ and inflated most, by up to
a factor of ten.
\item \textbf{In-sample residuals.} Our first heteroscedastic-GP comparison estimated
noise from in-sample residuals of a GP conditioned on the same points, which
underestimate it, and the het-GP lost on every dataset.
\item \textbf{A biased noise estimator in both input-dependent arms.} Moving to
out-of-fold residuals was not enough. An out-of-fold squared residual is noise plus
latent variance; the regional arm then added the latent variance again, and the
het-GP took a geometric mean of squared residuals, $0.28\times$ too small. Together
these produced a complete negative result (global noise best on nine of eleven
datasets, the het-GP on none) and a spurious sample-size reversal that an earlier
draft of this paper reported as a finding. With the estimator corrected, the result
is the opposite (\cref{sec:noise}).
\end{enumerate}

A comparison is only as meaningful as the effort spent on the arm one is not
advocating. Reporting, for every arm, what was fitted, what was selected, on which
split, and what the estimator's expectation is would have caught all five. The fifth
was found only by an independent audit of the code against the paper.

\subsection{A checklist for GP calibration studies}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Confirm the inference path is exact. Disable fast predictive-variance
approximations and raise any Cholesky-size threshold above $n$, for training as well
as prediction; verify that results below the threshold are unchanged by the setting.
\item Fit kernel hyperparameters in the space the kernel operates in, and verify a
fitted lengthscale against the median pairwise distance of the \emph{transformed}
inputs.
\item Give every arm the same tuning budget, and state what was fitted and selected
for each.
\item Estimate any residual-derived quantity out-of-fold, and check its expectation:
an out-of-fold squared residual includes latent variance, and the log of a squared
residual is biased by $-1.27$.
\item Screen datasets for the property under study over several seeds; do not assume
it. A dataset named for a phenomenon may not exhibit it:
\texttt{synthetic\_heteroscedastic} here ranks below five measured datasets
(\cref{tab:screen}).
\item Report a proper scoring rule alongside any binned calibration metric, and the
$\ece$ noise floor for the test-set size.
\item Average metrics across datasets only in scale-free form; CRPS, NLL differences
aside, is in target units, and one dataset can dominate a raw mean.
\item When sweeping $n$, hold the validation and test sets fixed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\begin{itemize}
\item Our constructed datasets reach het ratios of ${\sim}1600$ where measured data
averages at most ${\sim}9$ (\cref{tab:screen}), and vary noise in steps rather than
smoothly, which structurally favours a piecewise-constant noise model. The claims of
\cref{sec:noise} rest on the real-data results.
\item The heteroscedastic GP is a two-stage $k$NN construction, not a full
implementation of \citet{kersting2007} or of the variational heteroscedastic GP
of~\citet{lazarogredilla2011}. An independent GPyTorch implementation with a GP noise
model (\texttt{src/hetero\_independent.py}, five seeds) agrees where the effect is
large, improving on its own global baseline on airfoil ($0.077 \to 0.048$ $\ece$) and
calhousing ($0.068 \to 0.029$), but is worse on the other four, most of all on the two
smallest (energy and yacht). How much an input-dependent noise model gains therefore
depends on how it is built; that it gains on the larger heteroscedastic datasets does
not.
\item Residual variance that varies with $x$ may reflect model bias rather than noise
(\cref{sec:noise}, \texttt{varying\_smoothness}); we therefore use the term
\emph{input-dependent residual variance}.
\item Some datasets carry known issues (\cref{sec:empirical}, \texttt{data/README.md}):
duplicated SARCOS test rows, duplicate inputs in wine\_white, censored calhousing
targets, and two simulated datasets. Small test sets limit how finely $\ece$ can
separate arms.
\item Training-set sizes run from $n=184$ to $n=4800$. Behaviour at the $10^5$--$10^6$
scale where sparse approximations are most needed is untested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sparse GP approximation.} Inducing-point and spectral methods
\citep{quinonero2005, titsias2009, rahimi2007, williams2001, smola2000} reduce the
$O(n^3)$ cost by replacing the kernel matrix with a low-rank surrogate. This
literature is overwhelmingly concerned with the \emph{latent} term of~\cref{eq:var};
our observation is that predictive \emph{uncertainty} is usually dominated by the
other term, which is why methods that differ substantially in approximation quality
can be nearly indistinguishable in predictive standard deviation.

\paragraph{Locally adaptive and mixture-of-experts GPs.} The closest prior art
partitions the input space and fits separate models per region
\citep{rasmussen2002, tresp2000, deisenroth2015, gramacy2008}. Our results are not in
tension with it, and the distinction matters. Those methods give each region its own
\emph{kernel and noise hyperparameters}, so per-region predictive variance genuinely
differs; the mechanism we identify, and our noise-model results, are precisely why
that is the right design. What we rule out is the weaker variant (one global model,
with only \emph{approximation fidelity} varying by region), which leaves the dominant
term of \cref{eq:var} untouched. Treed GPs~\citep{gramacy2008} additionally learn the
partition, which our result on residual-variance scoring complements.

\paragraph{Heteroscedastic GPs.} Input-dependent noise has been modelled by a second
GP on log residuals~\citep{goldberg1997}, by EM~\citep{kersting2007}, and
variationally~\citep{lazarogredilla2011}. Our regional noise is a coarse, piecewise
approximation to these, and \cref{sec:noise} finds that even this coarse form
significantly improves calibration on four of our ten real datasets (five of eleven
including the constructed one). Our experience adds a practical caution for
two-stage constructions: both the latent variance in out-of-fold residuals and the
log-of-squared-residual bias are large enough to reverse a comparison.

\paragraph{Calibration in regression.} Interval-coverage calibration
\citep{kuleshov2018} and proper scoring rules~\citep{gneiting2007} measure different
things. In our final results they mostly agree (the exception is the het-GP on
protein, significantly worse on $\ece$ but significantly better on NLL and CRPS),
whereas an earlier, biased version of the comparison had them disagree
systematically, which is one reason we report both.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and measurement validity.} \Cref{sec:hazard} belongs to a
line of work showing that reported gains can originate in evaluation setup rather than
method~\citep{henderson2018, musgrave2020, lipton2019}. Our instance is unusual in
being a silent, threshold-triggered approximation inside a trusted library rather than
a protocol choice, which makes it invisible in code review.

\section{Conclusion}

Region-aware Gaussian processes can adapt two things by region: the fidelity of the
approximation, or the noise model. Only the second can change the predictive
uncertainty a region receives. Sparse approximations alter only the latent term of the
predictive variance, so models of very different fidelity emit predictive standard
deviations within $2\%$ of one another in every region of our constructed data, and an oracle
router given the true partition gains nothing ($+0.0003$ $\ece$). Making the noise
term regional, by contrast, improves calibration significantly on five of eleven
datasets and harms it on none, with the gains on datasets whose measured residual
variance is input-dependent and whose training sets are large enough to estimate it.

The practical readings are that routing approximation fidelity by region is not worth
building, that the same regional machinery pointed at the \emph{noise} model is worth
building when a screen shows input-dependent residual variance, and that the partition
such methods need is recoverable by residual-variance scoring when the regions differ
in residual variance.

Separately, practitioners measuring GP calibration should verify that their inference
path is exact. Above a library default that appears nowhere in user code, ours was
not, and every calibration number we computed above it before noticing was wrong,
by up to a factor of ten.
